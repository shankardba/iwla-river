Concern

Saltier streams hold fewer sensitive animals, well below EPA's chloride criterion

Where volunteers measured chloride within a week of a bug survey (644 surveys at 236 sites), saltier streams have fewer pollution-sensitive animals and more tolerant ones, with paving, season and state held fixed; scores start to fall around 47–60 mg/L, far below EPA's recommended long-term criterion of 230 mg/L.

40%of groups found are sensitive, chloride under 25 mg/L
25%at 100–230 mg/L
47 mg/Lwhere Virginia SOS scores drop

The Save Our Streams program asked whether chloride is linked to the bugs volunteers find: both the overall stream score and each kind of animal. Both sides here are volunteer data. Every bug survey is paired with chloride that volunteers measured at the same site, with an SOS chemistry kit (76% of pairs) or a Salt Watch strip, within 7 days. That gives 644 surveys at 236 sites, mostly in VA, NY, IA, MD, MN; median chloride 40 mg/L. Both survey forms are used: the Virginia SOS form (scored 0–12) and the original SOS form (0–44), so scores are put on one scale as a percent of each form's maximum.

Salt and pavement travel together (rank correlation 0.69 here), so every comparison below holds the paved share of the watershed, the season, the state and the survey form equal. The paved share comes from the national land cover maps; it is the only outside data used, and only as a control.

The mix of animals tracks chloride most clearly: fewer sensitive groups, fewer mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies, more tolerant animals. The overall score moves the same way but less sharply, since it blends several measures: on the Virginia form it drops 0.6 points per tenfold chloride (p = 0.04), on the original form 1.4 points (p = 0.10).

Within the same site, a saltier week does not come with different bugs. That is what one would expect if the animals reflect the salt a stream carries over months and years rather than on the day: comparing sites by their typical chloride gives the clearest link of all for sensitive groups.

Where the drop happens: a change-point search, which finds the chloride level that best splits high scores from low, puts it at 47 mg/L for the Virginia form (90% interval 25–54; mean score 8.6 below, 6.9 above) and 60 mg/L for the original form (48–165; 17 vs 11). Both are far below EPA's chronic criterion of 230 mg/L, which was set from laboratory toxicity tests on single species. Reviews of field studies find that stream communities can change at chloride levels below such criteria.

Stoneflies show the strongest link: on the Virginia form they are found in about half of surveys at 25 mg/L and far less often at 100. Common net-spinning caddisflies make up a smaller share of the catch as chloride rises, while midges and flatworms make up more. On the original form, most caddisflies are found less often and sowbugs make up more. Mayflies fall with chloride in the raw numbers, but that mostly goes once paving is held fixed. The other groups show no clear link either way.

Two more checks point the same way. Winter chloride at a site (December to March) against its spring bug survey: 185 surveys at 87 sites, sensitive share -5.1 points per tenfold, p = 0.08, in the same direction but short of a clear result. And EPA's national river survey, where professionals measured chloride and bugs on the same visit (8,752 visits): 34% of streams under 25 mg/L were in good condition, against 8% above 860 mg/L (rank correlation -0.31).

Limits. Paving is held fixed in every comparison, but salt and paving rise together, so the two cannot be fully separated: some of what is shown here may be other harms that come with development. Very salty samples are few (59 surveys at 9 sites above 230 mg/L). Salt Watch strips read in steps, and zero readings are left out as likely blanks.

What would strengthen this most: only 9% of bug surveys (644 of 7,296) have a chloride reading at the same site within a week. A chloride test at every bug survey, and at the same sites through the winter, would turn this into a much sharper answer.

How this was worked out

Pairs: each Virginia SOS rocky-bottom or original SOS bug survey with the median of volunteer chloride readings (SOS Chemical, Salt Watch; zeros and readings over 20,000 mg/L dropped) at the same site within 7 days (30 days for change points and animal groups). Least squares of each measure on log10 chloride with paved share, season, state and form, standard errors clustered by site. Same site: site and season means removed. Site's typical chloride: weighted least squares on site means with HC3 errors. Change points: nonparametric deviance reduction, 90% interval from 500 bootstrap resamples of whole sites. Animal groups: share of animals counted by least squares, presence by logistic regression, both with paving and season, clustered by site; q-values by Benjamini–Hochberg. Clean Water Hub snapshot of 2026-10-27; joined public data as of 2026-10-28. Code: iwla_river/salt_bugs.py.

Terms used on this page
False discovery rate, q-value
A correction for testing many sites or animal groups at once. A q-value below 0.05 means that, among all the results called real, no more than about 5% are expected to be flukes.
p-value
How often a link at least this strong would turn up by chance if there were really none. Below 0.05 is usually taken to mean chance is an unlikely explanation.
95% interval
The range the true value very likely lies in, given the data. If it runs from below zero to above zero, the direction of the effect is not settled.
Rank correlation (Spearman)
How closely two measures rise and fall together in rank order, from −1 (one always falls as the other rises) through 0 (no link) to 1.
Bootstrap
Re-running the analysis on many resamples of the data, taking whole sites at a time, to see how much the result could vary.
Change point
The chloride level at which the bug scores shift most sharply from one typical level to another.
EPT
Mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies (Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera): three groups that need clean water.
