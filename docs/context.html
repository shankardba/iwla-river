In context

The volunteer data checked against public measurements: agency laboratory chloride, professional bug surveys, state lists of impaired waters, land cover, permits and census data.

What each public dataset addedThe 20,384 Save Our Streams sites were joined to 20 public datasets from USGS, EPA, the Census Bureau, Maryland and county governments, so each survey can be read against its watershed, its weather and other people's measurements. Context Volunteer chloride tracks agency lab chlorideWhere a state or federal agency measured chloride in the same small watershed within a week, volunteer SOS chemistry readings agree closely (correlation 0.70; 86% within a factor of two); Salt Watch strips agree more loosely (0.47). Good news Chloride is rising at more sites than it is fallingOf 396 sites with at least eight chloride readings over four or more years, 69 show a rising trend and 12 a falling one after correcting for testing many sites at once. Watch Paved land, more than winter salt, tracks low bug scoresAcross 140 watersheds, saltier winters go with lower spring bug scores, but that link disappears once paved area is accounted for; paving remains a strong predictor (p < 0.001). Context Paving keeps spreading, fastest where volunteers workThe median watershed with an IWLA bug site went from 1.3% to 2.0% paved between 1985 and 2025, gaining more than other watersheds nearby (+0.6 vs +0.35 points); within a watershed, bug scores have not yet tracked that growth. Watch A storm the week before does not change a site's scoreComparing each site with itself, surveys taken in the week after high water score the same as calm-week surveys (-0.004 standard deviations, 95% CI -0.05 to +0.04). Good news State impairment listings match the bug surveysVolunteer sites in watersheds the state lists as impaired for aquatic life score lower (8 vs 10 on the Virginia SOS scale), and professional surveys show the same gap. Context Volunteers and professionals mostly agree, but volunteers rate generouslyWhere a Virginia SOS survey sits within 1 km of a Maryland state survey, the two agree on condition 67% of the time (weighted kappa 0.50), as well as Maryland's own trained volunteers do; volunteers more often call a stream healthier than the professionals. Context Most high-need watersheds have no volunteer monitorOf 1,093 watersheds with five or more pressures, 1,039 have no active volunteer bug site; 135 of those are within 25 km of one. Concern Stream restoration: no gain beyond what a placebo showsVirginia SOS scores near completed stream restorations rise +0.40 points more than at comparison sites, but a fake completion date five years earlier shows a rise just as large (+0.50). Context Three surveys capture most of what a site is64% of the variation in a bug survey's share of sensitive animals is the site itself, so the average of 3 surveys predicts a site's long-run average with reliability 0.84; past about six surveys, more visits add little. Context Maps predict about half of why sites differ; the rest is localMapped land, pollution, flow and weather data predict 46% of the differences between sites' average share of sensitive animals at sites the model never saw; most of the rest is local, and only a survey finds it. Context Urban watersheds stand apart; livestock watersheds score like low-pressure onesGrouped by their pressures alone, 286 small watersheds fall into 5 stable types: urban watersheds have the fewest sensitive animals (27% vs 40% in low-pressure ones), while livestock watersheds are no different from low-pressure ones. Concern

Data: Clean Water Hub (Izaak Walton League), snapshot 2026-10-27; USGS Watershed Boundary Dataset and NLDI; EPA StreamCat; Open-Meteo; US Census Bureau boundaries. No personal details are shown.
