Paved watersheds lose sensitive animals

Nationally, sensitive animals fall from 40% of groups found in near-natural watersheds to 22% where a quarter or more is paved.

 In contextGood news

Volunteer chloride tracks agency lab chloride

Where a state or federal agency measured chloride in the same small watershed within a week, volunteer SOS chemistry readings agree closely (correlation 0.70; 86% within a factor of two); Salt Watch strips agree more loosely (0.47).

 In contextWatch

Chloride is rising at more sites than it is falling

Of 396 sites with at least eight chloride readings over four or more years, 69 show a rising trend and 12 a falling one after correcting for testing many sites at once.

 In contextContext

Paved land, more than winter salt, tracks low bug scores

Across 140 watersheds, saltier winters go with lower spring bug scores, but that link disappears once paved area is accounted for; paving remains a strong predictor (p < 0.001).

 In contextContext

Volunteers and professionals mostly agree, but volunteers rate generously

Where a Virginia SOS survey sits within 1 km of a Maryland state survey, the two agree on condition 67% of the time (weighted kappa 0.50), as well as Maryland's own trained volunteers do; volunteers more often call a stream healthier than the professionals.

 In contextConcern

Most high-need watersheds have no volunteer monitor

Of 1,093 watersheds with five or more pressures, 1,039 have no active volunteer bug site; 135 of those are within 25 km of one.

10 findings

The volunteer data checked against public measurements: agency laboratory chloride, professional bug surveys, state lists of impaired waters, land cover, permits and census data.

What each public dataset addedThe 20,384 Save Our Streams sites were joined to 20 public datasets from USGS, EPA, the Census Bureau, Maryland and county governments, so each survey can be read against its watershed, its weather and other people's measurements. Context Volunteer chloride tracks agency lab chlorideWhere a state or federal agency measured chloride in the same small watershed within a week, volunteer SOS chemistry readings agree closely (correlation 0.70; 86% within a factor of two); Salt Watch strips agree more loosely (0.47). Watch Paved land, more than winter salt, tracks low bug scoresAcross 140 watersheds, saltier winters go with lower spring bug scores, but that link disappears once paved area is accounted for; paving remains a strong predictor (p < 0.001). Context Paving keeps spreading, fastest where volunteers workThe median watershed with an IWLA bug site went from 1.3% to 2.0% paved between 1985 and 2025, gaining more than other watersheds nearby (+0.6 vs +0.35 points); within a watershed, bug scores have not yet tracked that growth. Watch A storm the week before does not change a site's scoreComparing each site with itself, surveys taken in the week after high water score the same as calm-week surveys (-0.004 standard deviations, 95% CI -0.05 to +0.04). Good news State impairment listings match the bug surveysVolunteer sites in watersheds the state lists as impaired for aquatic life score lower (8 vs 10 on the Virginia SOS scale), and professional surveys show the same gap. Case study: Great Seneca Creek

13 findings

One creek followed in depth: the IWLA Rockville Chapter's two monitoring sites on Great Seneca Creek in Germantown, Maryland, surveyed since 2005. How stream health has moved, which animals are changing, road salt in winter, how the creek compares with what its setting predicts, and how nearby streams are changing.

Compared with what its setting predictsSite 1 finds fewer pollution-sensitive animals than its setting predicts, and the gap appears to be widening (p = 0.055, about 11 percentage points a decade). Watch The score has held steadyScores held near 16.5 before 2013 and 16.6 since; the overall score shows no trend. Steady The mix of animals is shiftingBetween 2005–2009 and 2020–2024, damselflies and common net-spinning caddisflies became rarer while dobsonflies and aquatic worms became regulars. Watch Trend testsAt Site 1 the share of pollution-sensitive groups is declining (p = 0.042) while the total score shows no clear trend. Concern What moves the scorePollution-sensitive animals drive the swings: they add 2 to 18 points per survey. Context Season mattersSpring surveys average 2.6 points more than summer ones, so trends must compare like seasons. Context The chapter changed survey forms in 2024Since 2024 the chapter has also used the Virginia form, which the Hub's own site page does not chart. Context Great Seneca Creek within its watershedsGreat Seneca Creek's subwatershed finds 39% sensitive groups against 39% expected; its wider watershed, Seneca Creek, finds 39%. Context Road salt reaches the creek every winterChloride in the creek climbs from about 85 mg/L in summer to winter peaks of 159, still below the EPA's 230. Watch Nitrate stays lowNitrate strips have read 2 mg/L at the creek on every visit. Good news Map of sites around the creek1652 Clean Water Hub sites with surveys lie within 30 km of the chapter; the long-record ones cluster to the southwest, across the Potomac. Context Streams near the creek are mostly improving11 of 31 long-record sites within 30 km show improving scores, the opposite of Site 1's direction. Good news Sites near the creek25 other Hub sites within 10 km have usable surveys, mostly salt and nitrate readings. Context
