How the Save Our Streams findings were worked out

Each key finding on the Save Our Streams record rests on a handful of methods. This page shows each method as a short animated diagram, then walks through every finding: what was joined, which methods ran, the numbers with their intervals, and what the finding can't tell you.

Dots in the diagrams are illustrative, drawn to look like the real data. Wherever a diagram or card says “real”, the numbers are the site's own (Clean Water Hub snapshot 2026-10-27).

Before the methods

Three questions behind every finding

Which rows? Volunteer data come from different forms, places and dates. Most analyses use only the surveys that could be paired with something else, so each finding says how many surveys and sites it stands on.

Compared with what? A link between two things can come from a third (paving brings both salt and lost habitat). The methods hold such things equal, or compare each site only with itself.

How sure? Every estimate carries an interval: the range a different but similar set of sites would likely give. An interval that crosses zero can't be sure of the direction.

Method 1 · Description

Averages by bin

The simplest picture: sort surveys by one thing, average another. It shows the shape of a relationship before any model, but it can't separate causes.

Method 2 · Joining data

Pairing in time and space

Bug surveys and chemistry readings are taken on different days and sometimes at different spots. A pairing rule says how close in time (and place) a reading must be to count, and the share that paired is reported first.

Method 3 · Confounders

Holding things equal

Multiple regression estimates the link between two variables while other variables are held fixed. Errors are “clustered by site”: a site's repeat visits count as one piece of evidence, not several.

Method 4 · Confounders

Each site against itself

Instead of naming every difference between sites, remove them all: subtract each site's own average (and each season's) and look only at how a site changes from visit to visit.

Method 5 · Uncertainty

How sure: intervals from resampling

The bootstrap asks what the estimate would be with a different, similar sample. Resampling whole sites (or whole watersheds) keeps their visits together. Regression intervals on the site use a formula that does the same job (cluster-robust errors).

Method 6 · Change over time

Trends at each site

Water quality swings with the seasons, so trends compare like seasons with like. Testing hundreds of sites calls for a false-discovery-rate correction.

Method 7 · Thresholds

Change points

Where does a response step down? The nonparametric change-point search (King and Richardson 2003) tries every split and keeps the one that explains the most.

Method 8 · Checking the measurements

Agreement with a reference

Before trusting volunteer readings, compare them with professional ones taken nearby: numbers on a log scale, ratings as a cross-table.

Method 9 · Noise

Reliability: how many visits make a site

A mixed model separates what belongs to the site from what changes visit to visit; the intraclass correlation (ICC) is the site's share.

Method 10 · Prediction

Predicting sites the model never saw

Machine-learning models fit anything; the honest test is prediction for sites held out, with each site's surveys kept together.

Method 11 · Grouping

Types of watershed

Clustering groups watersheds by their pressures alone, checks the groups are stable, and only then compares the bugs.

Method 12 · Overlay and count

A need score and coverage

Some findings are counts: overlay public maps of pressures with where volunteers work.

Afterword · Re-checking

How DataLab re-checks these findings

DataLab's study engine re-ran the road-salt analysis independently and got the same slopes. It also adds tests the site doesn't run, such as the shuffle test below. It gave “saltier streams hold fewer sensitive animals” a verdict of hinted: the link holds across sites, in each half of the data and against EPA's professional survey, but it vanishes when each site is compared with itself.
